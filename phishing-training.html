Lesson 1 of 10

What phishing really is

Phishing is a message pretending to be someone you trust, trying to get you to do something that helps an attacker. The disguises change constantly — the ask never does.

Watch for these four common requests. Attackers may also ask for confidential documents or personal information:

🔑Your password
💵Your money
✅Your approval
💻Your computer
Ignore the costume. Look at what the message is asking you to do. And remember — phishing arrives by email, text, phone call, and QR code. Same game, different envelope.
Lesson 2 of 10

"Too small to target" is a myth

Attackers run automated campaigns hitting thousands of businesses at once. Nobody has to choose us — being on the internet is enough.

  • Real money moves through real accounts — payroll, vendor payments, client funds.
  • Small teams mean fewer layers — often one person controls payments or IT.
  • Our inboxes are the prize — invoices, wire details, client documents, and trusted relationships an attacker can impersonate.
The good news: anyone can build this habit. It works alongside protections such as MFA, email filtering, updates, and clear payment-approval procedures.
Lesson 3 of 10 · Interactive

Anatomy of a phish

Below is a fictional example of a common phishing lure. The controls are safe training buttons, not real email links. Tap or click the parts that look suspicious. There are 4 to find — 0 of 4 found.

From:
Subject:

Your Microsoft 365 password is set to expire in 24 hours. To avoid interruption to your email and Teams access, please verify your account immediately.

Microsoft 365 Account Team

The real address doesn't match. The display name says "Microsoft," but the actual domain is m365-account-services.example — not microsoft.com. Anyone can set a display name to anything. Tap the sender's name to reveal the real address, especially on your phone.
Manufactured urgency. A countdown can pressure you into skipping verification. Real deadlines exist too; urgency is a reason to check independently, not proof of fraud.
Generic greeting. A generic greeting is a weak clue, not proof. Legitimate messages can be generic, and attackers can use your real name. Judge the request and verify independently.
The link is the trap. In a real email, inspect the destination without opening it. This training button has no destination. A convincing login page is not proof of safety; use your trusted bookmark or official app instead.
Lesson 4 of 10

Urgency is a weapon

Many scams manufacture a reason you cannot wait. Taking time to verify through a trusted channel can interrupt the attack.

  • "Within 24 hours" · "immediately" · "final notice"
  • "Are you at your desk? I need this handled quietly."
  • Fear ("account suspended") or reward ("refund waiting")
Reframe it: urgency is a cue to pause. Follow your established emergency process and verify through a trusted channel. A deadline does not justify skipping payment or security checks.
Lesson 5 of 10

Who is this really from?

Level 1 — Display name games

"Sarah (CEO)" can be typed by anyone. Always check the actual address — on mobile, tap the name to reveal it.

Level 2 — Lookalike domains

micr0soft.com (zero for O) · rnicrosoft.com ("rn" for "m") · company-billing.net (plausible extras).

Level 3 — The hard one: real address, hacked account

A vendor's genuine mailbox gets compromised, and the phish arrives from their real address, mid-conversation, in a real thread.

That's why sender-checking alone isn't enough. Unusual requests — new bank details, unexpected attachments, "log in here" — get verified through a different channel, even from known senders.
Lesson 6 of 10

Never let the message choose your route

  • Inspect the destination without opening it — hover on a computer or use your phone’s link-inspection controls. Avoid loading a page preview. When uncertain, use the official app or a trusted bookmark.
  • Unexpected attachment? Verify with the sender first — even a known contact. A quick call or Teams message.
  • A QR code is another way to open a link. Legitimate codes exist, but unexpected login or payment requests need independent verification. Do not open a destination just to inspect it.
The single most protective habit: if an email says "log in to your account," don't use their link. Open your browser and type the address you know — office.com, your bank, the carrier. Check for relevant notices there, or contact support through a known channel. Not every genuine notification appears in an account dashboard.
Lesson 7 of 10

The Big Four — always verify first

The request type, not the message's appearance, determines the risk. Highest alert whenever a message asks you to:

🔑Enter a password
💵Send money / change banking
✅Approve an MFA prompt
💻Install / open a file

These four earn the 30-second check every time — even if the email looks perfect, even if it's from the boss, and especially if it's urgent. This check matters even when the message has no spelling or design mistakes. Also verify requests for confidential business or personal data.

On MFA: a sign-in prompt you did not trigger may indicate an unauthorized sign-in attempt. Deny it and report it immediately. The prompt alone does not prove your password was stolen. Never share a sign-in code or approve a request to satisfy an unexpected caller.
Lesson 8 of 10

Same scams, different doors

ChannelWhat it looks like
📱 Text ("smishing")"Your package is held — pay a $1.90 redelivery fee." Check delivery or fee requests through the carrier’s official app or website, not the text link.
☎️ Phone ("vishing")"This is IT — read me the code on your screen." Do not share passwords or sign-in codes with an unexpected caller. Hang up and contact your help desk using its known number.
🔔 MFA fatigueA flood of "Approve sign-in?" prompts at 2 AM, hoping you'll tap Approve to make it stop. Never approve to silence it — call IT.
▦ QR phishingEmailed codes to "re-verify your account." Don't scan; sign in normally at office.com instead.
Text, call, prompt, or code — the same Big Four requests get the same verification.
Lesson 9 of 10

Well-meant instincts that make it worse

  • Don't reply — even to ask "is this real?" You'd be asking the attacker, and confirming your address is live.
  • Don't verify using contact info inside the message — that "helpful" phone number belongs to the scammer. Use numbers you already have.
  • Don't forward it around the office — that just gives the phish more chances to be clicked. Report it to one place.
  • Don't process banking or payment changes from email alone — ever. Voice verification on a known number, no exceptions for deadlines.
  • Don't delete it before reporting — the original email is evidence that protects your coworkers.
  • Don't click "just to see" — curiosity on a work computer becomes everyone's incident.
Lesson 10 of 10

If you already clicked: speed beats shame

Anyone can make a mistake — including IT professionals. Reporting quickly helps responders limit the damage. Do not wait for symptoms or hide the message. Even a prompt report may still require incident response.

What happenedDo this right now
Clicked, entered nothingReport it anyway — it protects coworkers who got the same email.
Entered your passwordCall IT immediately. From a trusted device, use the official site to change the password (and any reused copies). IT may also need to revoke sessions and check account changes.
Approved a rogue MFA promptCall IT now — unauthorized access may have been granted. IT needs to review sign-ins and revoke suspicious sessions.
Opened a suspicious file / ran softwareFollow your incident procedure. If malware is suspected, disconnect Ethernet and Wi-Fi, leave the device on, and call IT from another device. Do not keep investigating on that computer.
Sent payment / changed bankingCall the bank’s fraud line immediately using a known number and request a stop or recall. Notify leadership and IT. Recovery is not guaranteed; speed matters.
Scanned a QR + entered infoReport what you entered. For passwords, follow the credential steps above; for card or bank details, contact the bank immediately.
Speed beats shame: reporting is the responsible action, even when you made the mistake or time has passed. Leaders should encourage a no-blame reporting culture. Your organization sets its own policy; this course cannot promise a particular disciplinary outcome.

Think you clicked something? What to do now

Your reporting route

Use your employer’s Report Phishing button or designated IT/security contact. If you do not know the process, contact your manager or IT provider through a number you already trust.

Entered a password, approved a prompt, ran a suspicious file, or sent money? Call immediately using your organization’s urgent reporting process. Do not wait for an email reply. For payment fraud, contact the bank’s fraud team immediately too.

Tell the responder

  • When it happened and which device or account was involved.
  • The sender, subject, and what the message asked you to do.
  • What you clicked, entered, downloaded, approved, or paid.

Keep the original message and follow your reporting procedure. Do not circulate it to coworkers. Never include passwords or one-time sign-in codes in a report.

Your Stop. Check. Report. checklist
BlueStrata • Desk reference

Stop. Check. Report.

1. Stop when the request matters

Passwords, money, MFA approvals, downloads, or confidential data deserve an independent check. Urgency and secrecy are cues to pause.

2. Check through a trusted route

Use the official app, a trusted bookmark, or a number already in your records. Do not use contact details supplied in the suspicious message. Verify payment changes through your established approval process.

3. Report before you delete

Use your company’s Report Phishing button or IT/security contact. If you entered information, approved access, ran suspicious software, or sent money, call immediately. Explain exactly what happened.

My IT/security contact: ___________________________
Urgent reporting number: ___________________________

A familiar sender, polished wording, HTTPS, or a convincing voice is not proof. AI can make impersonation more believable. Verification still matters.
